ABSTRACT
Fairness in the recommendation domain has recently attracted
increasing attention due to more and more concerns about the
algorithm discrimination and ethics. While recent years have wit-
nessed many promising fairness aware recommender models, an
important problem has been largely ignored, that is, the fairness
can be biased due to the user personalized selection tendencies or
the non-uniform item exposure probabilities. To study this problem,
in this paper, we formally define a novel task named as unbiased
fairness aware Top-N recommendation. For solving this task, we
firstly define an ideal loss function based on all the user-item pairs.
Considering that, in real-world datasets, only a small number of
user-item interactions can be observed, we then approximate the
above ideal loss with a more tractable objective based on the in-
verse propensity score (IPS). Since the recommendation datasets
can be noisy and quite sparse, which brings difficulties for accu-
rately estimating the IPS, we propose to optimize the objective
in an IPS range instead of a specific point, which improves the
model fault tolerance capability. In order to make our model more
applicable to the commonly studied Top-N recommendation, we
soften the ranking metrics such as Precision, Hit-Ratio, and NDCG
to derive a fully differentiable framework. We conduct extensive
experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of our model based
on four real-world datasets.

1 INTRODUCTION
Recommender system, as an effective remedy for information over-
loading, has greatly facilitated people’s daily life and production.
In the past few decades, improving accuracy has long been the
major objective for the recommender models. However, recom-
mender system basically aims to serve human beings, thus ethical
concerns cannot be ignored, among which fairness is an impor-
tant aspect [ 27] and has recently attracted increasing attention.
To build fairness aware recommenders, people have proposed a
lot of strategies, for example, [ 37] defines four types of fairness
constraints to regularize the matrix factorization model. [ 31, 32]
achieve fairness by removing the sensitive information based on
adversarial training. [19] considers the personalization of the users
and defines counterfactual fairness based on causal notions. [12]
pulls the performances between the advantaged and disadvantaged
groups through distribution matching.
While these models have achieved many successes, an important
problem has been largely ignored, that is, the fairness can be biased
due to the non-uniform observational datasets. As illustrated in
Figure 1, suppose we have two user groups A and B. Ideally, a fair
model should provide equal recommendation performance for the
users in A and B on all the items. However, in real-world scenarios,
the observed datasets are usually biased. For example, suppose
the users in the system all like horror movies better than comedy
ones. Then, in their interaction records, we can observe a much

higher percentage of horror items. In this case, if we directly impose
fairness constraints on the observed data, the performance gap on
the (underrepresented) comedy movies may be not well controlled,
and if we evaluate the performance on the comedy movies, the
results for group A and B can be not fair. Similar phenomena may
also happen in the bottom case, where the interactions of group
A are more about digital products, while the users in group B are
more interested in beauty items. By a traditional fairness aware
model, we may lower the performance gap between group A and
B on the digital and beauty items, respectively. However, for the
clothes, which are seldomly purchased by group A and B, there is
no explicit constraint to guarantee that the performances between
them are fair.
In order to alleviate the above problem, in this paper, we propose
to build an unbiased fairness aware recommendation framework
(called UFAR for short). Our general idea is to leverage the inverse
propensity score (IPS) [22] to downweight the user-item pairs with
higher observational probabilities, and assign larger weights to
the underrepresented samples. Based on such weight adjustments,
different samples are expected to have fair rights to represent the
group performance. While this seems to be an interesting idea,
there are still many challenges: to begin with, while IPS has been
widely studied in many domains, such as causal inference, com-
puter vision, etc. It is still not clear how to use IPS to debias the
fairness. And then, the recommendation datasets are usually noisy
and sparse. Directly estimating IPS based on such data can be not
reliable, which may lead to sub-optimal recommendation and fair-
ness performances. How to design a more reliable IPS estimation
mechanism may also challenge our idea. To overcome these chal-
lenges, we firstly define an ideal fairness objective. Then, to make
this objective optimizable, we propose an unbiased objective for
the observational datasets based on IPS. In our model, we do not
expect the IPS directly estimated from the dataset to be absolutely
correct. Instead, we assume that the real and predicted IPS can be
bounded within a small range. With this assumption, we firstly
find the IPS which can lead to the largest loss in the above range,
and then minimize this loss to learn the model parameters. By this
strategy, we actually optimize the objective based on a range of
IPS instead of a specific point, which can better resist the nega-
tive impact brought by the incorrect IPS estimation. In addition, to
make our framework more adaptive for Top-N recommendation,
we smooth the ranking metrics such as Precision, HR, and NDCG to
measure the performance gaps between different user groups, based
on which we build an end-to-end framework to achieve unbiased
fairness aware Top-N recommendation.
In summary, the main contributions of this paper can be con-
cluded as follows:
• we analyze the potential biases of the recommendation fairness
in observation datasets1, which to the best of our knowledge, is the
first time in the recommendation domain.
• To achieve the above goal, we firstly define an ideal fairness-
aware learning objective, and then approximate it by an unbiased
target for easy optimization. We also improve our model by a range-
based IPS strategy and soften the ranking metrics to adapt Top-N
recommendation.
• We conduct extensive experiments to demonstrate the effec-
tiveness of our framework.
2 PRELIMINARIES
2.1 User-oriented Fairness
Fairness in the recommendation domain can be item-oriented or
user-oriented, where the former cares more about the item exposure
opportunities [16], while the latter aims to reduce the performance
gaps between different user individuals or user groups [30]. Accord-
ing to the fairness measurement, user-oriented fairness includes
individual-level fairness [20] and group-level fairness [ 10]. Usu-
ally, individual-level fairness requires that similar users should be
treated similarly[30, 32], while group-level fairness aims to lower
the performance discrepancy between different user groups. In this
paper, we focus on user-oriented group-level fairness. Formally,
suppose we have a user set U and an item set V, all the interac-
tions of a user 𝑢 ∈ U are collected in a item set V𝑢. Suppose 𝑠 is
the sensitive feature, indicating different user groups2, then group-
level fairness requires that the average performance gap between
different user groups should be small enough, which is defined as
follows:

3 THE UFAR MODEL
In this section, we firstly define an ideal objective for fairness aware
recommendation. Then, to make this objective optimizable, we pro-
pose an unbiased loss function based on IPS for easy optimization.
To make the loss more reliable, we introduce a range-based strategy
to enhance the IPS estimation. At last, we further improve the loss
with soft ranking metrics for adapting the Top-N recommendation
task, which is more practical for real-world scenarios.

4 RELATED WORK
Our method is related with the fields of fairness aware recommen-
dation and debiased recommendation. In the following, we briefly
review these fields, and discuss the relation between our paper and
the previous work.
4.1 Fairness Aware Recommendation
Fairness aware recommendation can be classified into item-oriented
and user-oriented methods. In the item-oriented methods, the model
is usually optimized to provide equal exposure opportunities for
different items [ 16]. In the user-oriented methods, people have
defined individual-level fairness and group-level fairness. In this
paper, our focus is the user-oriented group-level fairness. In the
past few decades, there are a lot of promising models proposed
along this line. For example, [37] proposes four types of fairness
constraints on the matrix factorization model. [17] designs a model
to remove the mainstream bias to achieve the recommendation
fairness. [32] leverages adversarial training to learn the user em-
beddings which cannot predict sensitive information. [19] defines
personalized counterfactual fairness based on causal notions, and
builds an adversarial model to achieve it. [ 18] provides a post-
processing strategy to achieve fairness aware recommendation by
solving an integer programming problem. While the above models
have achieved many successes, they mostly focus on the models.
However, in our paper, we consider fairness from the data perspec-
tive and build an unbiased fairness aware recommender framework,
which significantly differs from the previous work.
4.2 Debiased recommendation
To overcome the data bias problem in the recommendation, people
have proposed a large amount of debiased recommender models.
For example, [25] uses propensity score to reweigh different train-
ing samples for building an unbiased learning objective, which is
demonstrated to be quite effective for correcting the data bias. To
remove the bias of the user implicit feedback, [24] proposes an IPS
strategy to revise the cross entropy loss. [ 22] proposes an asym-
metric tri-training framework based on two predictors to correct
the data bias. [3] proposes a framework to achieve robust learn-
ing based on the reweighing mechanism. [ 7] proposes a doubly
robust estimator to alleviate the high variance issues brought by
the traditional imputation models. Most of the above methods aim
to improve the overall model performance by correcting the bi-
ases of the user-item interactions. However, we care more about
the fairness problem between different user groups. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first work on debiased fairness aware
recommendation.

5.2 Overall Comparison
The overall comparison results can be seen in Table 2, from which
we can see: compared with the original models, the best debiased
method can usually achieve better overall recommendation per-
formance, which agrees with the previous work [2, 3, 24, 34]. The

reason can be that the original models are optimized based on the bi-
ased training set, without debiasing strategies, they cannot perform
well on the unbiased testing sets.
While FOCF and UFAR- both have fairness constraints, we find
that their fairness performances are not satisfied. In many cases,
their performances on PG are even worse than the original model’s.
We speculate that in FOCF, the fairness is evaluated based on the
rating prediction performance, which is not adaptive for the ranking
metrics in PG. For UFAR-, the fairness is computed based on the
performance of the observational biased dataset, whose distribution
differs from the testing unbiased dataset. As a result, both of their
performances are not high.
Encouragingly, our framework can obtain the best performance
on all the metrics, and the results are consistent on almost all the
datasets and original models. Between different implementations of
the fairness constraint, the soft ranking strategy is better because
it is more aligned with the evaluation metrics. More specifically,
compared with the original model, our framework can improve
the performance by about 5.070%, 5.596%, 65.92%, and 35.50% on
NDCG@10, F1@10, PG(NDCG@10) and PG(F1@10), respectively.
Actually, the results are not surprising. The purely debiased mod-
els cannot regularize the performance gaps between different user
groups, and the IPS in these models is directly estimated from the
noisy recommendation datasets. However, in our framework, we
explicitly regularize the performance gap and introduce a range-
based strategy to predict more reliable IPS, which helps to achieve
better performance. Compared with the fairness aware baselines,
our framework can simultaneously correct the biases in the rec-
ommendation model and fairness constraint, which leads to better
performances on different metrics.
If we observe the recommendation performances on different
user groups, we find that on Yahoo, ML-1M, and WeChat, the per-
formances of the disadvantaged users can be significantly improved
by slightly sacrificing the advantaged user performances. For the
Coat dataset, the performance of the male group is enhanced, while
the female user performances are lowered. It seems that, for all the
datasets, the performance improvement on one group is usually
larger than the damage on the other group performance. Thus, our
framework can improve the overall performance in most cases.
5.3 Ablation Studies
Our framework is composed of many components. A natural ques-
tion is whether these components are all necessary, and if yes, how
do they contribute to the final performance? To answer this ques-
tion, we conduct many ablation studies. In specific, we compare
our framework with its four variants: in UFAR (w/o F), we drop
the fairness constraints in objective (11). In UFAR (w/o L), we drop
𝑝0 (𝑜𝑢,𝑣 = 1) and 𝑝1 (𝑜𝑢,𝑣 = 1), and only use the initially estimated
IPS to correct the bias. In UFAR (w/o I), we do not estimate the ini-
tial IPS, and only learn 𝑝0 (𝑜𝑢,𝑣 = 1) and 𝑝1 (𝑜𝑢,𝑣 = 1) without any
range based constraints. In UFAR (RF), we build fairness constraint
based on the rating prediction performance. By UFAR (w/o F), we
aim to evaluate whether the fairness constraint in our framework is
effective. By UFAR (w/o I) and UFAR (w/o L), we would like to study
whether the designed range-based IPS estimation strategy is useful.
By UFAR (RF), we hope to investigate whether the ranking based

fairness constraint is effective. Since the main focus of our paper
is improving the recommendation fairness, we evaluate different
models based on PG in terms of NDCG@10 and F1@10, respectively.
We use the soft ranking to implement the fairness constraint due
to its higher performance. The experiment is conducted based on
the datasets of Yahoo and WeChat, and we use MF as the original
model. The conclusions on the other datasets and original models

We present the results in Figure 2, where we can see: UFAR
(w/o F) performs much worse than UFAR on all the datasets and
evaluation metrics, which is as expected, and demonstrates that
the fairness constraint is indeed necessary to lower the perfor-
mance gap between different user groups. If we drop 𝑝0 and 𝑝1,
the performance of UFAR (w/o L) is not satisfying compared with
UFAR. The reason can be that by learning 𝑝0 and 𝑝1, we optimize
the “worst-case” loss induced by the IPS in a set. Once the real
IPS falls into this set, our framework ensures that the lower bound
performance is not worse. If we only optimize the model based on
a specific IPS, then performance cannot be guaranteed once the IPS
is not accurate. As a result, we observe that UFAR can exhibit better
performance. However, only using 𝑝0 and 𝑝1 is still sub-optimal,
which is evidenced by the lowered performance of UFAR (w/o I) as
compared with UFAR. We speculate that blindly learning 𝑝0 and
𝑝1 can be too ambitious to efficiently discover the true IPS. If we
introduce a prior to guide their searching scope, then the results
can be better. In our framework, we use the initially estimated
IPS as the prior, which is shown to be effective. When we build
the fairness constraint based on the rating prediction performance,
our framework cannot perform well on the fairness metric for the
Top-N recommendation task. The above results demonstrate that
all the components are necessary, and they play different roles to
contribute to the final performance, where neither one of them can
fully dominate the other one.

5.6 Parameter Analysis
In this section, we study the influence of hyper-parameters on the
final fairness performance. More specifically, we focus on the tem-
perature 𝜏 in the soft ranking metrics and the balancing weights 𝜆
and 𝛾. The other experiment settings follow the above experiments.
Influence of 𝜏. In our soft ranking mechanism, 𝜏 determines
the softness of the probability that an item is ranked at a position.
Extremely, if 𝜏 → ∞, then an item is uniformly ranked at different
positions. When 𝜏 → 0, the soft-ranking mechanism is reduced
to the traditional hard ranking metrics. In our experiment, we
tune 𝜏 in the range of [0.1, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16]. From the results presented
in Figure 4, we can see: the performance fluctuates severely as
we use different 𝜏’s, and the changing patterns highly depend on
the datasets and evaluation metrics. A consistent observation is
that the best performance is usually achieved when 𝜏 is moderate.
Too large and small 𝜏’s are both sub-optimal. We speculate that
too large 𝜏 may lower the ranking significance between different
positions, which may make the model hard to separate the positive
and negative samples. However, if we set𝜏 as a too small value, the
model may rely on the observed dataset too much, which can also
be inferior, since the observed dataset may contain too much noise
and randomness. The above results suggest that 𝜏 is a sensitive
parameter for our framework, and in practice, it is important to
tune it carefully for achieving satisfying performance.

6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this paper, we propose an unbiased fairness aware Top-N recom-
mendation framework. The unbiasedness is achieved based on IPS,
and to improve its accuracy, we introduce a range-based strategy.
For building the fairness constraint, we soften the ranking metrics
to evaluate the performance gap between different user groups.
Extensive experiments are conducted to verify the effectiveness
of our framework. This paper actually makes a first step towards
unbiased fairness aware recommendation. There is still much room
left for improvement. For example, one can extend our framework
to multiple sensitive features or multi-value sensitive attributes.
In addition, it is also interesting to extend our framework to the
sequential and graph settings, where the current item is also biased
by the neighbor users or items.

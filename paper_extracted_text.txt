1. Introduction
Online learning platforms provide students access to interactive learning materials without geographical con-
straints, making them increasingly popular in education. To enhance learning effectiveness in these platforms,
personalized learning has emerged as a hot research topic, where exercises are adaptively recommended with an
appropriate level of difficulties. A key challenge in personalized learning is aligning recommendations with students’
evolving learning states. Knowledge tracing (Abdelrahman et al., 2023), which forms the foundation of exercise
recommendation, models learning dynamics and predicts future performance using sequential models. Based on the
estimated knowledge states, exercise recommendation aims to provide exercises that effectively support learning in
line with students’ cognitive needs.
Existing personalized exercise recommendation approaches mainly fall into two categories: collaborative filtering
(CF)–based methods (Li et al., 2021; Ngo et al., 2024) and learning-based methods (Cheng et al., 2026; Yang
et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2019; Xian et al., 2019). Collaborative filtering is a widely adopted technique in educational
recommendation scenarios. It generates recommendations by identifying similarities between learners or exercises,
showing advantages such as simplicity and ease of application even in the absence of explicit instructional knowledge
structures. However, despite their efficiency, these approaches often rely on shallow behavioral data (e.g., learner
response logs). They fail to capture students’ deeper cognitive states or trace how those states evolve, leading to
recommendations that lack strong educational relevance (Guan et al., 2025).
In contrast, learning-based recommendation approaches, such as deep reinforcement learning (Cai et al., 2019;
Cheng et al., 2026), recommend items based on students’ evolving knowledge states, which are inferred by knowledge
tracing (KT) models (Wang et al., 2025a; Gao and Liu, 2025) from their past interaction logs. However, a disconnect

Recently, knowledge graphs have been increasingly integrated into personalized learning recommendation sys-

relationships among students, exercises, and knowledge concepts, thus can offering interpretable recommendation

different abilities. Neglecting these attributes may lead to inaccurate recommendations and incomplete interpretability
by overlooking essential exercise characteristics.
To overcome these limitations, this study formulates personalized exercise recommendation as a multi-relational
cognitive knowledge graph construction and inference problem. We propose 2CKG4ER, a content-aware cognitive
knowledge graph-based recommendation method. Specifically, exercise semantic features are extracted from textual
descriptions using a BiGRU model, while cognitive attributes, including difficulty, discrimination, and learner overall
ability, are estimated using item response theory. Learner mastery levels over knowledge concepts which are derived
from knowledge tracing models and forgetting patterns which are modeled by exponential decay are incorporated as
relational features. Based on both cognitive relations, recommendation relations are further constructed to capture
personalized student–exercise interactions, forming the content-aware cognitive knowledge graph. The heterogeneous
entity and relational features are then separately fused to learn unified graph representations. Finally, we introduce
RsConvE, a relation-specific extension of ConvE that restricts the prediction space during link inference, to estimate
student–exercise recommendation scores and generate personalized exercises.
The main contributions are as follows.
∙ Bridging the gap between knowledge tracing and exercise recommendation. Existing learning-based
recommendation methods estimate student knowledge states while largely overlooking exercise characteristics,
creating a mismatch between learner representations and recommendation targets. We address this issue by
incorporating exercise semantic and cognitive features into both student state modeling and recommendation,
enabling recommendations that are better aligned with students’ evolving knowledge states and learning needs.
∙ A 2CKG4ER method based on content-aware cognitive knowledge graph. We designed a feature extraction
module, upon which the cognitive knowledge graph is built. A multi-source feature representation module then
fuses features, followed by a knowledge graph embedding technique RsConvE to obtain recommendation scores.
The recommendation reasons can also be provided based on the entity and relation features.
∙ Empirical validation and interpretability analysis. We compare 2CKG4ER with eight baseline recommen-
dation methods on three educational datasets. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method
achieves competitive adaptivity, with an approximately 2.9% improvement over the second-best baseline, while
maintaining stable novelty performance. Moreover, 2CKG4ER provides interpretable recommendation reasons
to enhance the transparency of personalized exercise recommendations.

2.1. Personalized Exercise Recommendations
Exercise recommendation plays a crucial role in improving students’ learning efficiency in online learning systems.
Existing methods can be broadly classified into two categories: collaborative filtering-based methods and learning-
based methods.
Collaborative filtering-based (CF) methods recommend exercises based on similarities among students or exercises.
Walker et al. (2004) utilized learner response similarities for recommendation, while Thai-Nghe et al. (2010)
applied matrix factorization to predict learner performance using user-item-rating triplets. Subsequent studies have
incorporated additional student information, such as knowledge levels, learning styles, trustworthiness, and social
ranking information, to enhance recommendation accuracy (Dwivedi and Bharadwaj, 2011; Segal et al., 2014; Wu
et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2019). Although CF-based methods are simple and effective, they mainly rely on behavioral
similarities from interaction data, failing to capture the complex, multi-relational structures among educational entities
(e.g., students, exercises, and knowledge concepts). Furthermore, these methods typically overlook pedagogical factors
and semantic contexts inherent to the learning process, including the semantic textual features of exercises, students’
cognitive states and forgetting behaviors, and exercise difficulty. Consequently, they struggle to generate deeply
personalized recommendations or offer pedagogical explanations for their decisions (see a summary in Table 1).
To improve educational personalization, learning-based methods use knowledge tracing (KT) models to estimate
learners’ knowledge states for generating tailored recommendations. For instance, Minn et al. (2018) combined deep
KT models with K-means to dynamically cluster learners. Recently, reinforcement learning (RL)-based methods have
emerged as a prominent trend in exercise recommendation, using leverage predicted knowledge states as reward signals
to optimize exercise sequencing (Cheng et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2019). For
example, Liu et al. (2019) utilized the Deep Knowledge Tracing model (Piech et al., 2015) to simulate the environment
by modeling student cognitive dynamics, subsequently applying RL to optimize recommendation strategies. Similarly,
Cheng et al. (2026) adopted an actor-critic algorithm for exercise recommendation, using large language models
to construct prerequisite and similarity graphs among concepts. Despite their performance, existing approaches
typically rely on ID-based embeddings for graph entities and relations, neglecting valuable semantic content. To
alleviate this issue, (Ozyurt et al., 2025) integrated a semantically grounded KT model to enrich question and
knowledge concept representations with semantic information under a reinforcement learning framework. However,
despite these advances, RL-based models suffer from black-box decision-making, lacking transparency regarding their
underlying reasoning (see a summary in Table 1). To bridge this gap, this paper introduces an interpretable exercise
recommendation method powered by a multi-relational cognitive knowledge graph.
2.2. Knowledge Graph Embedding
A knowledge graph is a multi-relational graph in which nodes represent entities and edges represent relations among
entities. It is typically expressed as triples in the form <head entity, relation, tail entity>. Due to their capability of
representing structured factual information, knowledge graphs have been widely applied in recommendation systems
(Xian et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). However, their complex structures and vast scales present significant computa-
tional challenges. To facilitate efficient representation and downstream reasoning, knowledge graph embedding (KGE)
techniques are widely employed. KGE maps entities and relations into low-dimensional vector spaces while preserving
their semantic properties and structural topologies (see (Cao et al., 2024) for a recent survey).
Various KGE techniques have been introduced using different scoring functions for estimating the triple plausibility
scores. From this perspective, existing techniques can be classified into three groups: distance-based models, semantic
matching models, and neural network models. 1) Distance-based models, a representative approach, define scoring
functions based on geometric distances in the vector space. For instance, TransE (Bordes et al., 2013) models relations
as translations between entity embeddings, while TransH (Wang et al., 2014) projects entities onto relation-specific
hyperplanes to handle complex relational patterns. RotatE (Sun et al., 2019) further extends this idea by modeling
relations as rotations in complex space, enabling effective handling of symmetry and inversion. Building on this,
ReflectE (Zhang et al., 2022) in recent studies introduces reflection transformations over relation-specific hyperplanes
to better capture the same relational patterns. DensE (Lu et al., 2022) models relations using non-commutative rotations
and scaling in SO(3) space to capture compositional and hierarchical semantics. CompoundE3D (Ge et al., 2023)
unifies translation, rotation, and scaling operations into compositional geometric transformations for more expressive
relation modeling. 2) Semantic matching models design scoring functions based on similarity or bilinear interactions
in latent vector spaces. Representative methods include RESCAL (Nickel et al., 2011) and DistMult (Yang et al.,
2015), which model relation-specific similarity through bilinear factorization, and ComplEx (Trouillon et al., 2016)

2.3. Interpretable Recommendation
Interpretable recommendation has drawn considerable attention in recent years (see (Zhang and Chen, 2020)
for a survey). By offering the rationale behind algorithmic decisions, it enhances user satisfaction and trust in
recommendation systems (Ma et al., 2024). Early interpretable approaches mainly focused on incorporating user
features or item side-information to generate explanations, employing techniques such as attention mechanisms and
recurrent neural networks (RNNs) (Dong et al., 2017). For example, Dong et al. (2017) generated product reviews from
attribute-level information using neural models to produce coherent explanations. In recent studies, knowledge graphs
have been increasingly utilized as they are helpful for generating intuitive explanations for recommended items (Xian
et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). Because KGs contain rich information about users and items, they can effectively model
complex relationships. Specifically, Xian et al. (2019) used multi-hop reasoning paths in knowledge graphs to justify
and explicitly explain recommendation results. Yang et al. (2022) introduced contrastive learning for robust entity and
relation representation in knowledge graph-based recommendation.
Although these approaches are effective in the e-commerce domain, they face limitations when applied to education
for two reasons: (1) they fail to extract educational features from the learning process, and (2) multiple types of
entities are involved, and the relationships among them are more complex than in the e-commerce domain. To address
these issues, Guan et al. (2023, 2025) incorporated students’ learning features, such as knowledge mastery levels, the
likelihood of the subsequent concept, and forgetting behaviors, into knowledge graph-based recommendation. They
created the knowledge graph with three entity types: knowledge concepts, students, and exercises. While this method
provided interpretable exercise suggestions, it relied on ID-based representations of entities and relations, limiting its
ability to capture rich semantic content (e.g., the textual content of exercises) and fine-grained pedagogical factors,
such as learners’ overall proficiency, exercise discrimination, and difficulty variations (see a summary in Table 1). To
this end, we extract semantic content from exercises and capture pedagogical factors for both students and exercises.

3.2. Problem statement
Definition 3. Personalized learning recommendation aims to suggest exercises that align with students’ cognitive
needs, as inferred from their historical learning records, to enhance learning efficiency. Formally, given a recommen-
dation size 𝑁, the task is to select a subset 𝐸 ⊆ 𝐸 containing 𝑁 exercises that the student has not yet practiced.
𝑆
In the context of the cognitive knowledge graph, the recommendation task is formulated as a missing edge
prediction problem. Given the head entity 𝑠 (a student) and the relation 𝑅𝑟 (recommendation), the goal is to predict
a tail entity (exercise 𝑒) that best matches the student’s current cognitive states. To this end, a scoring function is
introduced to evaluate the plausibility of each triple (𝑠, 𝑅𝑟, 𝑒), indicating the matching degree between the student and
the exercise. These scores are then used to rank candidate exercises and generate the final recommendation list.
4. Method
This section presents the proposed 2CKG4ER for interpretable personalized exercise recommendation based on
the content-aware cognitive knowledge graph. As shown in Figure 2, 2CKG4ER consists of four core modules: feature
extraction, cognitive knowledge graph construction, feature representation, and recommendation. (1) Initially, the
feature extraction module extracts features from the learning process, including entity features (the semantic content
of exercises and cognitive parameters) and relational features (mastery and forgetting). (2) Building on these extracted
features, the construction module extract recommendation triples. The constructed content-aware cognitive knowledge
graph  composed of three entity types (students, exercises, and knowledge concepts), and three relationship types,
including mastery, forgetting and recommendation. (3) The representation module learns the representation of entities
and relations by using an MLP operator to fuse multi-modal features. (4) The recommendation module calculates
the recommendation scores between students and exercises via ReConvE, a variant of the ConvE knowledge graph
embedding model, and infers the reasons behind the recommendation decisions from the knowledge graph .
4.1. Feature Extraction

students. Many exercise recommendation methods treat exercises merely as indices, overlooking these crucial entity
features. This neglect makes recommendation less practical, as exercises associated with the same knowledge concept
can vary significantly in textual semantics, difficulty levels, and discrimination. In contrast, our proposed 2CKG4ER
method encodes these entity features to ensure recommendation practical and personalized.

4.1.2. Feature Extraction for Relationships
This component extracts two types of cognitive relation features, cognitive mastery and cognitive forgetting. (1)
Cognitive mastery reflects students’ evolving knowledge states, which are obtained via a knowledge tracing (KT)
model from their historical interaction sequences. Inspired by MMKT (Wang et al., 2025b), we develop an Exercise
Content-Aware KT (ECAKT) model that incorporates exercise textual semantics and cognitive parameters. We exclude
the image modality and text-image fusion module because only a small proportion of exercises contain images.
Unlike conventional KT models that solely rely on knowledge concept IDs, ECAKT preserves exercise-level semantic
information. This design bridges the gap between concept-level cognitive state modeling in KT and exercise-level
recommendation, enabling 2CKG4ER to better distinguish exercises associated with the same knowledge concept but
varying in semantic content, difficulty, and discrimination, thereby improving personalized exercise recommendations.
(2) Cognitive forgetting quantifies the rate at which a learner fails to recall previously attempted exercises. It is
calculated using an exponential decay function based on the elapsed time intervals.
The proposed recommendation method is not limited to this specific knowledge tracing model. The cognitive
knowledge graph builds on the generated features of nodes and edges independent of the internal implementation of
the chosen knowledge tracing model (for mastery). Therefore, other advanced knowledge tracing models can also be
integrated into our proposed method if they provide learner-state estimates of mastery and forgetting.

5.1. Datasets
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method across different educational recommendation scenarios, we
conduct experiments on three public educational datasets: Eedi-sub, Algebra 2005, and XES3G5M-sub. These datasets
contain questions from different subject domains and include both English- and Chinese-language data. All datasets
contain student response records and exercise-knowledge concept associations, which are required for constructing the
cognitive knowledge graph.
• Eedi-sub is derived from the Eedi2020 dataset released in the NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge (Wang
et al., 2020, 2021). The original Eedi2020 dataset contains 1,382,727 responses from 4,918 students on 948
multiple-choice mathematics exercises. For the exercise recommendation task, we retain learners whose response
sequence length is between 10 and 200, resulting in the Eedi-sub dataset.
• Algebra 2005 is from the KDD Cup 2010 Educational Data Mining Challenge (Stamper et al., 2010), which
contains step-level tutoring records with correctness labels and knowledge-component annotations.
• XES3G5M-sub is derived from the a large-scale mathematics XES3G5M benchmark dataset (Liu et al., 2023),
which has 18,066 learners, 7,652 questions and 865 knowledge concepts. To keep computationally feasible, we
construct XES3G5M-sub by keeping sequences whose length is between 10 and 400.

5.2. Baselines
We compare the proposed 2CKG4ER method with eight baseline methods from two categories: five KGE-based
methods (including TransE-ER, TransE-adv-ER, RotatE-ER, DistMult-ER, and ComplEx-ER) and three traditional
CF-based recommendation methods (including EB-CF, SB-CF, and CBF). These five KGE-based methods use three
relational features (mastery, the likelihood of the subsequent concepts, and forgetting) by ID-based embeddings as
introduced in Guan et al. (2023, 2025), whereas CF-based methods use only interaction sequences. The KGE-based
baselines provide a direct comparison with existing knowledge graph embedding approaches, while the CF-based
baselines evaluate the effectiveness of incorporating knowledge graphs to exercise recommendation. All methods are
evaluated using the same dataset partition.
• TransE-ER (Guan et al., 2025): TransE models relations as translation vectors from head entities to tail entities.
In this baseline, TransE is trained on the cognitive knowledge graph by converting continuous relation features
into discrete relation types and relying on ID-based embeddings.
• TransE-adv-ER (Guan et al., 2025): TransE-adv enhances TransE with self-adversarial negative sampling,
assigning higher weights to hard negative triples with larger prediction scores to learn more discriminative
embeddings.
• RotatE-ER (Sun et al., 2019): RotatE models relations as rotations in the complex vector space, examining the
effectiveness of rotational relation modeling for cognitive graph-based exercise recommendation.
• DistMult-ER (Yang et al., 2015): DistMult is a bilinear semantic matching model that scores triples through a
diagonal bilinear product. It provides a representative real-valued semantic matching baseline for graph-based
exercise recommendation.
• ComplEx-ER (Trouillon et al., 2016): ComplEx extends bilinear modeling to the complex vector space and
captures asymmetric relations. This baseline evaluates complex-valued relation representations for learner-
exercise recommendation.
• EB-CF (Sarwar et al., 2001): Exercise-based collaborative filtering recommends exercises based on exercise
similarity derived from learner response histories. It serves as a non-KG baseline based on behavioral similarity.
• SB-CF (Liu and Hao, 2012): Student-based collaborative filtering recommends exercises based on the behavior
of similar learners. It represents a learner-neighborhood recommendation strategy without knowledge graph
information.
• CBF (van Meteren and van Someren, 2000): Content-based filtering recommends exercises based on exercise-
knowledge concept associations. It ranks candidate exercises by matching their knowledge concepts with the
learner’s historical knowledge profile.

5.4. Implementation Details
We split all datasets at a ratio of 75% and 25% for training and testing, respectively. In the feature extraction
module, the target mastery level 𝜎 and the target forgetting level 𝜎 are both set to 0.8. We chose a higher value
𝑚 𝑓
of 𝜎 to favor easier exercises. According to Papoušek et al. (2016), easy exercises yield better learning outcomes
𝑚
during shorter study sessions ( 10% error rate), whereas harder exercises ( 50% error rate) require longer engagement
periods. Regarding 𝜎 , since short-term retention gradually builds long-term memory (Kitamura et al., 2017), we set
𝑚
this parameter to prioritize recommending exercises that students are most at risk of forgetting. For each student 𝑠
𝑖
in the training set, the top-10 exercises with the smallest distances 𝑊 (see Equation (8)) are selected to construct
𝑖,𝑗
positive recommendation triples (𝑠 , 𝑅 , 𝑒 ). When training the 2CKG4ER, we generate five negative samples for each
𝑖 𝑟 𝑗
positive recommendation triple by randomly replacing the tail entity with an unpracticed exercise. The existing positive
cognitive triples remain unchanged during this process.
The entity and relation embedding dimension is set to 200. Accordingly, the inputs to the convolutional layer in
RsConvE are reshaped into 20 × 10 2D feature maps. The batch size is 1024, the learning rate is 0.001, and the model
is trained using the Adam optimizer for 25 epochs. The dropout rates for the input layer, hidden layer, and feature maps
are set to 0.2, 0.2, and 0.3, respectively. During testing, all recommendation triples are excluded from the evaluation
graph to avoid data leakage. The recommendation scores for unobserved student-exercise pairs are computed solely
the embeddings generated from the trained feature-fusion layers via Equation (18).
The embedding dimension of the other five KGE-based baselines is 1000, following Guan et al. (2025)’s work.
All KGE-based methods are performed five times on each training dataset using five different random seeds (namely
2024, 2025, 2026, 2027, and 2028), and we report both the mean and standard deviation of the results. The CF-based
methods are run once under the same data partition since they are deterministic. We set the recommendation length
to 𝑁 = {5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50}. All experiments are implemented in Python using PyTorch and NumPy,
and the source code of our 2CKG4ER is available at https://github.com/Tangfgjk/KG4ER.

5.5.3. Interpretable Evidence (RQ3)
In order to demonstrate the interpretability of the proposed 2CKG4ER, we utilized the quantitative (questionnaires)
method to assess the helpfulness and reasonableness of recommendation reasons in exercise selection.
Questionnaires: Inspired by (Guan et al., 2025), we designed three questionnaires (see https://github.
com/Tangfgjk/KG4ER). Each questionnaire depicts a student’s ability profile, the recommended exercise, and the
recommendation reasons. In particular, the recommendation reasons comes from the following four factors: namely
mastery cmkc, forgetting cfr, exercise difficulty diff, and exercise discrimination disc, which are the extracted features
in Section 4.1.
• Mastery: representing a student’s master level over knowledge concepts,
• Forgetting: showing a student’s forgetting rate on an exercise,
• difficulty, representing the difficulty level of an exercise, which shows whether the recommended exercises match
a student’s mastery level,
• discrimination, representing whether the exercise can distinguish students’ ability.
The questionnaire assess recommendation reasons from two aspects: namely helpfulness and reasonableness. Each
aspect contains four inquiry questions, which correspond to the four factors of the recommendation reason. Therefore,
each questionnaire contains eight inquiry questions.
• Helpfulness: Participants evaluated the helpfulness of the recommendation reasons (mastery, forgetting, diffi-
culty, and discrimination) in guiding their exercise selection using a five-point Likert scale, where 1 denotes no
assistance and 5 denotes substantial assistance.
• Reasonableness: Participants assessed the logic of the recommendation reasons by rating their reasonableness
on a five-point Likert scale, where 1 denotes the least reasonable and 5 denotes the most reasonable.
Participants: A total of 35 post-graduate students were involved to the three questionnaires via social networks
(i.e., QQ from Tencent). Among the participants, 18 of them responded to questionnaire I, and 17 of them responded
to questionnaires II-III, respectively.
Results analysis: Since the three questionnaires presented different student ability profiles, recommended ex-
ercises, and corresponding recommendation reasons, we first investigated whether variations in student profiles
affected participants’ evaluations. Specifically, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed to compare the scores of
the eight questionnaire items among the three questionnaires. The results (see Table 10) demonstrated that none of
the questionnaire items exhibited significant differences across the three questionnaires (p>0.05), with the minimum
p-value being 0.112. These findings indicate that the differences in student ability profiles did not introduce significant
evaluation bias. Consequently, the responses from all three questionnaires were aggregated for subsequent analysis,
resulting in 52 valid responses in total.

all four factors are greater than 4 (5 indicating the full points), representing that participants generally held positive
opinions for recommendation reasons (mastery, forgetting, difficulty, and discrimination). Specifically, forgetting and
exercise discrimination received the highest scores regarding helpfulness, whereas mastery and exercise difficulty are
most highly rated factors for reasonableness. This finding may be attributed to the different roles of these factors
in exercise recommendation. Forgetting rate is directly related to the intuitive need for review, making its helpfulness
easier for users to perceive. Exercise discrimination helps users understand whether an exercise can accurately evaluate
their current ability, leading to high evaluations in both helpfulness and reasonableness. Although mastery was
considered a highly reasonable factor (59.6% selecting “very reasonable”), its perceived helpfulness was relatively
lower (48.1% selecting “very helpful”), possibly because users were more concerned with identifying what to practice
next rather than understanding what they had already mastered. Moreover, Friedman test did not reveal significant

vided by the proposed 2CKG4ER. The consistently high evaluation scores indicate that the generated recommendation
reasons are understandable and effectively reveal the factors influencing exercise recommendations, demonstrating the
interpretability of the proposed 2CKG4ER.

6. Conclusion
In this study, we proposed 2CKG4ER, interpretable personalized exercise recommendation method based on a
content-aware cognitive knowledge graph. By incorporating shared semantic and cognitive exercise features across
both student modeling (ECAKT) and exercise recommendation, we bridge two traditionally separate tasks to enhance
recommendation performance, directly addressing the limitation of conventional models that fail to propagate exercise
properties from knowledge tracing to recommendation. Based on the extracted features of entities and relationships,
we constructed an attributed cognitive knowledge graph. We then adopted an MLP operator to fuse features from
multiple sources and devised the ReConvE knowledge graph embedding model to learn the interactions between
students and exercises. Experimental results demonstrate that 2CKG4ER achieved superior performance across three
datasets compared to eight baselines. While ReConvE operates as a latent embedding-based model, the proposed
2CKG4ER provides clear recommendation rationales by using rich entity and relational features in the attributed
cognitive knowledge graph.

Although 2CKG4ER achieves strong performance, it has a few limitations. First, our feature representation relies
on basic concatenation and MLP operators, which could be upgraded to advanced attention-based or hypergraph fusion
mechanisms. Second, student profiles are currently modeled using only the cognitive ability parameter, leaving room
to incorporate multi-dimensional learner behaviors. In the future, we plan to enhance the feature fusion architecture
and enrich learner profiles to further improve the performance of 2CKG4ER.
